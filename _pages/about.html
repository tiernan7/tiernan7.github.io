I develop systematic approaches for discovering, understanding, and engineering complex physical and biological systems.

Many important systems cannot be understood or optimized exhaustively: they are only partially observable, difficult to model, governed by competing objectives, and expensive to probe experimentally. My work asks how better abstractions, experiments, design principles, and automation can transform these problems from ad hoc arts into cumulative scientific and engineering disciplines.

My doctoral research with Chris Thachuk at the University of Washington focused on robust molecular programming and DNA nanotechnology. I developed computational and experimental strategies for improving DNA strand-displacement systems through multi-objective design optimization, circuit architecture, sequence design, and chemically expanded genetic alphabets. My broader work spans structure learning, mechanism-forward functional bioinformatics, experimental optimization, and scientific automation.

Across these areas, I view discovery and engineering as a reinforcing cycle. Mechanistic understanding enables better technologies, while engineered interventions and experimental platforms reveal new mechanisms. I am interested in building the principles and processes that make each cycle of learning, design, and experimentation more informative, reliable, and cumulative.

Before beginning my doctoral work, I studied chemistry, mathematics, and interdisciplinary biomedicine at the University of Massachusetts Amherst. My undergraduate research in structural DNA nanotechnology, protein biophysics, and synthetic biological systems first drew me toward the relationship between information, physical mechanism, and control.
